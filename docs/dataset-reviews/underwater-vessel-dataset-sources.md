# ShipsEar carries v1.0, Wolfset stays peripheral

ShipsEar is the only one of the two datasets suited to AcousticWise v1.0, but its licence is unresolved, so the public repo must ship code and a download script and no audio. The paper describes **90 field recordings of 15 s to 10 min, 11 vessel types plus background noise, recorded at 52,734 Hz, 24-bit** ([ShipsEar paper, Applied Acoustics 113, doi 10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008)). It ships as whole-event files, so recording-level splits (PRD M3) are feasible. Its terms are only "freely available to researchers" on request, with no stated licence, so PRD D1 resolves to its fallback: a download script and no audio. Wolfset is the opposite case. It has a clear **CC BY 4.0** licence ([figshare 25791978](https://api.figshare.com/v2/articles/25791978)) but contains no vessels at all. It is a 5-hour anechoic-tank recording of four outboard motors and one electric model-boat motor, with no vessel-type labels. It should not be the primary dataset and is a weak, potentially misleading P2 cross-source test; I recommend relabelling it as an out-of-domain demo. The next steps are to email the ShipsEar maintainers before the Oct 10 gate in the PRD, build the split from the original files and metadata, and verify DeepShip, Oceanship and QiandaoEar22 before choosing the P2 source. DeepShip is the leading candidate, but none of the three has a verified licence.

## ShipsEar fits the task but offers few, imbalanced recordings

The recordings were made in autumn 2012 and summer 2013, mostly in and near the port of Vigo and the Ria de Vigo on Spain's Atlantic coast. Bottom-moored hydrophones (MarSensing digitalHyd SR-1, flat 1 Hz to 28 kHz, 100 Hz high-pass in the preamp) were used, and the best of up to three channels was kept per record ([ShipsEar paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008)). The 11 vessel types are fishing boat, trawler, mussel boat, pilot ship, tugboat, dredger, ro-ro, ocean liner, passenger ferry, sailboat and motorboat, plus four background-noise recordings (wind, rain, waves, current). The authors merge these into five classes: A (fishing boats, trawlers, mussel boats, tugboats, dredgers), B (motorboats, pilot boats, sailboats), C (passenger ferries), D (ocean liners and ro-ro) and E (noise). The paper's Table 1 lists 16, 17, 27, 9 and 12 files for A through E, and the authors' leave-one-out GMM reached **75.4%** on a balanced 69-file subset, with class D worst at 55.5%.

The size figures do not reconcile. Table 1 sums to **81 files and about 170 minutes**, against the headline 90 files and the roughly 3.1 hours quoted in later literature ([arXiv 2609.29179](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29179)). That later paper also gives the wrong sampling rate (64 kHz), so the original paper should be trusted. Per-type counts for the 11 raw types are not in the paper. The pipeline therefore has to count from the actual files, and the "about 90 recordings" wording in the PRD risk table should be treated as approximate.

The PRD says classes are "ShipsEar's own" but does not say whether that means 5 merged classes or 11 types. I recommend the five A-E classes. With 11 types, several would have only a handful of recordings, and a recording-level test set would be near-empty per class. Even with five classes, class D has about 9 files, so one or two test recordings per fold. A single split would give unstable numbers. The PRD already calls for stratified splits and multiple seeds; I would strengthen that to repeated stratified grouped cross-validation, reporting the spread.

## No stated licence means D1 resolves to a download script with no audio

The landing page, redirected from the paper's URL, is live as of 2026-10-09. It offers an 83.9 MB sample zip of four WAVs and says: "To get full access to the database please send a request to the email contact below" (dsantos at gts.uvigo.es), with a footer of "(c) Uvigo-Sonitum 2016" ([landing page](https://underwaternoise.atlanttic.uvigo.es/), [samples.zip](https://underwaternoise.atlanttic.uvigo.es/samples.zip)). Neither the site nor the paper names a licence, redistribution terms or a required citation. The paper's wording is "made available to the research community" ([ShipsEar paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008)). My reading is that this is permission on request, not an open licence, and that nothing found permits committing the audio to a public repo. The researchers found no official mirror on Zenodo, Kaggle, Hugging Face or GitHub. Any community mirror has unknown provenance, so avoid it. I did not test whether the email address is still answered. The site is a single-maintainer 2016 PHP page last modified in 2022, so access and link-rot risk is real for a repo meant to be reproducible.

The practical consequences for D1 are as follows. `DATA.md` should record: source URL, the paper citation (Santos-Dominguez, Torres-Guijarro, Cardenal-Lopez and Pena-Gimenez, Applied Acoustics 113, 2016), the access date, the absence of a stated licence, and the date and outcome of the email request. The repo ships a script or instructions that point to the Vigo site, plus split manifests keyed by record id. It does not commit WAVs or derived clips. The sample zip is an unresolved question: it is publicly offered, but "samples" is not a redistribution grant, so the safe path is to ask in the same email whether the demo clips may be redistributed. If not, the PRD's P1-DEMO already allows clips the author may share. Because the PRD gate is "licence confirmed by Oct 10", the email should go out today, and the "unclear" outcome should be pre-accepted as the no-audio fallback instead of waiting for a reply.

## Original files allow recording-level splits, but only by vessel and date

The original distribution is one file per event or pass-by, "segmented with wide margins", and the authors themselves evaluated at file level ([ShipsEar paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008)). That makes M3 (no source recording in more than one split, enforced by an automated test) achievable if the pipeline assigns each recording to a split first and cuts windows afterwards. The widely circulated derived version is the opposite: a fixed split of pre-cut 5-second clips (1,778 train, 445 test) in which source-recording identifiers are unavailable, so recording independence cannot be verified ([arXiv 2609.29179](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29179)). That version must be avoided. The published accuracies above 99% on this benchmark are probably inflated by clip-level splits (my inference, not shown directly), so a correct recording-level result will look lower. That is a portfolio strength if it is framed honestly, and the PRD already demands a residual-leakage discussion for unusually high numbers.

The PRD's open question 4 (can metadata split by session or day, not only by file?) is answerable in principle. The record fields include id, vessel name, type, GPS location, date and time, channel depth and distance bin ([ShipsEar paper](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apacoust.2016.06.008)), and the live table at [indexDB.php](https://underwaternoise.atlanttic.uvigo.es/indexDB.php) exposes them. Sample filenames follow `<id>__<DD_MM_YY>_<name>.wav`, so id and date can be read from the filename. Three leakage routes remain beyond the same-file case. First, the same ship (a ferry on a daily route) can appear in several recordings, so groups should be vessel name, and ideally date. Second, site is confounded with class: the dredger was recorded at Punta Langosteira and noise at the Intecmar station, so a model can learn the place, not the vessel. Third, the 100 Hz recorder high-pass and variable gain are fixed quirks of one recorder. Held-out-site splits are impossible for some classes, so these effects can only be documented, not removed. Nobody has yet pulled the full 90-row metadata table, which is the concrete next task once audio arrives. Check there whether any records are consecutive pieces of one long event.

For the leakage test in M3 and the success metric, key the check on grouped identity, not only file: assert zero shared record ids and zero shared vessel names across train, validation and test, and report both.

## Wolfset is open and clean but is a different task

Wolfset (Lobo, Pessanha Santos and Moura, Sci Data 12:1323, 2025) holds **168 mono, 44.1 kHz, 16-bit PCM WAV files totalling about 4.97 hours and 1.5 GB**, recorded in the 8 x 5 x 5 m anechoic tank at the Lisbon Naval Base ([figshare 25791978](https://api.figshare.com/v2/articles/25791978), [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12311032/)). Filenames encode motor state, transient and noise codes (four outboard combustion motors, one electric model-boat motor, plus air bubbling, hoses, mallet strikes, air-rifle shots). There are no cargo, tanker, ferry or tug labels anywhere, and the authors do not list vessel-type classification among intended uses. A file-level count of the index CSV (done by the researchers) gives 109 single-motor files, 46 with no motor and 13 multi-motor. Licence is clean: CC BY 4.0, public, no login, DOI resolving, direct downloads with MD5 checksums, so the fetch script can verify integrity. Because licensing allows redistribution with attribution, Wolfset is the one dataset that could provide shareable demo clips, but only for the engine-classification demo, not for ShipsEar classes.

For P2 it is a poor fit. No defensible mapping exists between five engines in a tank and ShipsEar's classes. The researchers' nearest suggestion, motors 1 to 4 loosely as class B (small craft), is a stretch. Every file that has no motor also carries added bubbles, hoses or transients, so there is no genuine ambient class either. A ShipsEar-trained model would fail on Wolfset because of the lab-versus-harbour domain gap (finite tank, close range, 50 Hz mains hum, per-file gains of 20 to 40 dB), and that failure would say nothing about generalisation to other vessels. Leakage risk between the two sources is low because they share no authors, site or hardware, and the recording dates are not stated. Within Wolfset, repeat takes (x01 to x05) of one configuration are near-duplicates, and the only identity unit is the motor, so grouping must be by configuration or motor. If P2 uses it at all, the PRD should call it an "out-of-domain stress test" and not a cross-source generalisation claim.

## Implications for D1 and P2, and next actions

D1 as written ("Use ShipsEar. Record license, attribution and source in `DATA.md`. If redistribution is not permitted, ship a download script and no audio") is satisfiable now, but the working assumption should be flipped: redistribution is not permitted unless the maintainers say otherwise. The D1 evidence for the DoD would be the `DATA.md` entry, the email thread and the manifest. P2 as written ("cross-source evaluation on a second dataset, clearly separated from the same-source test") needs a tighter definition: the second source must have vessel-type labels that map onto the first, otherwise only a binary or coarse test is possible. A tank dataset cannot meet that. The researchers' background-knowledge description of DeepShip (the only verified detail is 4 classes: cargo, passenger, tanker, tug; 265 vessels; 609 readable recordings; about 47.2 hours; the 2026 paper splits at recording level before segmentation, licence not given) is an obvious P2 candidate ([arXiv 2609.29179](https://arxiv.org/html/2609.29179)). Its classes overlap only partly with ShipsEar's, so even DeepShip supports an independent-source test of the method (retraining on each source and comparing recording-level results), not a zero-shot transfer test, unless a coarse mapping such as ferry and liner to passenger, and tug to tug, is defined in advance.

The unverified candidates are DeepShip, Oceanship ([arXiv 2401.02099](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.02099)) and QiandaoEar22 (specific-ship identification on [IEEE DataPort](https://ieee-dataport.org/documents/qiandaoear22), [arXiv 2406.04353](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.04353)). For each, the follow-up should check the licence and access (IEEE DataPort commonly requires an account, which affects a scripted download), the presence of recording or vessel identifiers for grouping, class taxonomy overlap with ShipsEar, and whether the distributed form is whole recordings or pre-cut clips. I have only search snippets for Oceanship, and its PDF could not be parsed, so its figures should not be relied on. My suggested order of work is as follows.

1. Send the ShipsEar request email today. Ask for the full data, licence or terms, a preferred citation, and permission to share sample clips.
2. Obtain the original WAVs and extract the full metadata table. Count files per type, then settle on A-E versus 11 types, and decide the grouping key (vessel name plus date).
3. Build the grouped split, the zero-overlap automated test, and the repeated-CV report. Record the 90-versus-81 discrepancy in `DATA.md`.
4. After the Week 3 gate, check DeepShip first, then Oceanship and QiandaoEar22, and decide the P2 source. Keep Wolfset as an optional engine-ID demo, relabelled as out-of-domain.

## Conclusion

The sharper conclusion is that the two papers answer different questions. ShipsEar is the right data with the wrong access terms, and Wolfset is the right terms with the wrong data. Licence cleanliness and task fit are inversely related across these two, so the repo's honest story is a no-audio reproducible pipeline on ShipsEar, with the data question documented, not hidden.

Two things change the PRD. First, M3 as a file-level check is not enough; ShipsEar's vessel names mean the grouping can and should be tighter, which also answers open question 4 at the design level. Second, P2 should not be scheduled until a labelled field dataset passes a licence and identifier check; the verification of DeepShip, Oceanship and QiandaoEar22 is still open. Not yet known: whether the Vigo maintainers will answer or grant redistribution, the real per-type file counts, and the licences of all three follow-up datasets.
